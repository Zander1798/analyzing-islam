Entry Page — Abrogation, Chapter 1 (The Quran)
The Quran · Chapter 1 · Abrogation xxx
"Whatever verse We abrogate or cause to be forgotten" — the Quran acknowledges its own revision
Abrogation Scripture Integrity Strong Q 2:106
"We do not abrogate a verse or cause it to be forgotten except that We bring forth one better than it or similar to it. Do you not know that Allah is over all things competent?"

What the verse says

Q 2:106 states explicitly that Allah abrogates — cancels or replaces — his own previously revealed verses. The verse acknowledges that Quranic content can be forgotten or superseded, and presents this as a demonstration of divine power. This is the primary textual basis for the classical Islamic doctrine of naskh (abrogation).

Why this is a problem

If the Quran is the timeless, perfect word of an omniscient God, it is not clear why earlier divine pronouncements would need to be revised. An omniscient deity would know at the time of first revelation what the final ruling should be. The need to replace verses implies either that the initial verse was imperfect, or that God's will changed over time — both of which challenge the standard attributes of Islamic theology. The verse's own answer — that the replacement is "better or similar" — implies the original was suboptimal, requiring correction.

The Muslim response

Muslims argue that abrogation reflects divine wisdom in gradually preparing humanity for the full implementation of Islamic law — just as a teacher moves from simpler to more complex instruction. Abrogation is pedagogical, not corrective. Allah always knew the final ruling; the gradual reveal was for human benefit.

Why it fails

The pedagogical argument reframes the problem without resolving it: if gradual introduction was the plan, why commit the suboptimal intermediate stages to a document Islam declares perfect and eternal? The Quran itself is presented not as a lesson plan but as kitabun la rayba fih — "a Book in which there is no doubt" (Q 2:2). Preserving superseded instructions in an eternal, doubt-free text is precisely what requires explanation. The pedagogical defence concedes that some verses carried only temporary validity while others are permanent — a distinction the text itself does not mark, leaving Muslims dependent on later scholarly consensus to determine which verses remain operative.

Entry Page — Warfare & Jihad, Chapter 15 (The Quran)
The Quran · Chapter 15 · Warfare & Jihad xxx
"Kill the polytheists wherever you find them" — the Verse of the Sword
Warfare & Jihad Strong Q 9:5
"And when the sacred months have passed, then kill the polytheists wherever you find them, and capture them and besiege them and sit in wait for them at every place of ambush."

What the verse says

Q 9:5 — the "Verse of the Sword" in classical Islamic jurisprudence — commands believers to kill polytheists after the expiry of a four-month grace period, without restriction to defensive conflict. Classical jurists cited it as abrogating the more lenient earlier verses permitting peaceful coexistence, on the basis that it was among the last major revelations concerning relations with non-Muslims.

Why this is a problem

The verse contains no defensive precondition. "Wherever you find them" is an unrestricted command covering all circumstances of encounter. The instruction to besiege and ambush — offensive military tactics — further removes any defensive framing. The most influential classical commentators, including Ibn Kathir and al-Tabari, read it as a blanket command abrogating earlier peaceful verses.

The Muslim response

Contemporary apologists argue that Q 9:5 must be read in its historical context — a specific treaty violation by the Quraysh — and that the verse immediately following (Q 9:6) instructs that polytheists seeking protection must be escorted to safety. The command is therefore contextually bounded, not universal.

Why it fails

Q 9:6's protection clause applies to those who seek asylum before combat — it does not limit the scope of Q 9:5 itself. The historical-context argument also undermines Islam's claim that the Quran is a timeless document of universal guidance: if Q 9:5 is context-specific, the mechanism for distinguishing context-specific verses from universal ones is entirely external to the text.

Jizya in humiliation — "until they give the jizyah while they are subdued"
Warfare & Jihad Governance Strong Q 9:29
"Fight those who do not believe in Allah or in the Last Day and who do not consider unlawful what Allah and His Messenger have made unlawful and who do not adopt the religion of truth from those who were given the Scripture — until they give the jizyah while they are subdued."

What the verse says

Q 9:29 commands offensive warfare specifically against People of the Book (Jews and Christians) until they pay the jizya tax in a state of subjugation. The phrase wa hum saghirun — "while they are subdued" or "while they are humiliated" — is not incidental: classical jurists prescribed specific modes of collection designed to enact the humiliation the verse mandates.
