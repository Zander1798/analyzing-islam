Foreword
What this is

This book is a reference catalog of passages from Islam's foundational texts that present philosophical, historical, moral, or logical difficulties. Every entry cites a specific verse or hadith, explains what it says in plain language, and builds the case for why it presents a problem. Where relevant, entries also present the standard Muslim apologetic response and push back on it.

Volume I covers three sources: the Quran, Sahih al-Bukhari, and Sahih Muslim — the texts that Sunni Islam itself ranks as most authoritative. No fringe collections. No hostile translations. The case is built entirely from Islam's own mainstream tradition.

How entries are organized

The book is divided into three parts, one per source. Within each part, entries are grouped into thematic chapters — Abrogation, Contradictions, Warfare & Jihad, and so on. Each chapter collects every entry from that source that belongs to that theme. If a source has no entries in a given category, that chapter is omitted entirely.

When an entry touches more than one theme, it appears under the category that best captures its primary problem. It does not appear twice.

How to read an entry

Each entry contains four elements:

ReferenceThe source citation — verse number, hadith number, or book and chapter.
RatingThe apologetic difficulty level: Basic, Moderate, or Strong.
PassageThe text quoted in full from the standard translation.
CommentaryAn explanation of the problem — what it says, why it matters, and where the standard apologetic falls short.
vii
Foreword
Strength ratings

Every entry is rated according to how difficult the problem is to answer from within the Islamic apologetic tradition:

BasicApologists have a stock reply. The problem is real but the standard response is widely known and rehearsed. A reader who has followed Islamic apologetics will have heard the answer.
ModerateAnswering requires conceding something — softening a claim, reinterpreting a text, or acknowledging that the tradition is not unanimous. The problem cannot be dismissed outright.
StrongThe apologetic moves themselves generate new problems. Every standard response either contradicts another Islamic claim, requires abandoning the plain meaning of the text, or introduces a difficulty worse than the original.

Ratings reflect apologetic difficulty — not moral severity. A passage can be morally disturbing and still rated Basic if the apologetic reply is well-established and coherent. Conversely, an apparently minor textual quirk may rate Strong if no clean answer exists.

Sources and translations

Quranic verses are quoted from the Saheeh International English translation — the Saudi-sanctioned mainstream Sunni edition, widely used in mosques and Islamic universities across the English-speaking world. Choosing the most mainstream translation removes the easy dismissal of "hostile translation."

Hadiths are quoted from widely published English translations of the same collections that Sunni jurisprudence itself treats as authoritative: Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim in this volume. Hadith numbers follow the standard USC-MSA / sunnah.com numbering used in English-language Islamic scholarship. Every passage can be independently verified.

viii
Foreword
A note on tone

This catalog does not argue. It presents. The entries speak through the texts themselves — the reader is left to draw their own conclusions. No passage is fabricated, paraphrased to distort, or stripped of context that would change its meaning. Where context matters, it is provided.

The commentary aims to be precise rather than polemical. Where Islamic scholars disagree among themselves, that disagreement is noted. Where a passage has a defensible reading, that reading is acknowledged before the problem with it is explained. The goal is not to mock but to examine — carefully, specifically, and without concession.

Readers who find a specific entry inaccurate, mistranslated, or missing essential context are encouraged to raise the objection at analyzingislam.com, where every entry in this volume is also published online and open for scrutiny.

How to use this book

Read it in order or jump directly to a category. Each entry stands on its own. The thematic chapters are self-contained — no prior entry is assumed when reading any later entry.

Use the Quran Verse Index at the back to locate entries by surah and verse. Use the Hadith Reference Index to locate entries by collection and number. Use the General Index to find entries touching a specific topic, person, or concept across all three sources.

Every entry references a specific passage. Verify before citing.

Source Introductions — Parts I, II & III
Part I — Source
The Quran

The Quran is the central scripture of Islam, believed by Muslims to be the literal, verbatim word of Allah as revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibreel (Gabriel) over a period of approximately twenty-three years, beginning around 610 CE and concluding at Muhammad's death in 632 CE.

Muhammad himself could not write. The revelations were memorised by companions and written on whatever materials were available — bones, leather, palm leaves. Following Muhammad's death, the first caliph Abu Bakr ordered a written collection to preserve the text. A standardised version was later produced under the third caliph Uthman ibn Affan around 650 CE. Uthman ordered all variant manuscripts burned, establishing a single authoritative text — an act that remains theologically significant and historically contested.

The Quran is divided into 114 chapters (surahs), each composed of individual verses (ayat). The surahs are arranged roughly from longest to shortest, not chronologically. Islamic scholarship divides them into Meccan revelations (earlier, more theological) and Medinan revelations (later, more legislative and often harsher in tone). Where the two conflict, the doctrine of abrogation (naskh) holds that the later verse supersedes the earlier.

All verses in this volume are quoted from the Saheeh International English translation — the Saudi-sanctioned mainstream Sunni edition used across English-speaking mosques and Islamic universities worldwide.

Part II — Source
Sahih al-Bukhari

Sahih al-Bukhari is the most authoritative hadith collection in Sunni Islam, compiled by Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (810–870 CE), a Persian scholar born in Bukhara in present-day Uzbekistan. Sunni Muslims regard it as the most authentic book after the Quran itself — a status it has held for over a thousand years.

Al-Bukhari reportedly examined approximately 600,000 hadith traditions over sixteen years of travel across the Islamic world, retaining only those he judged to meet his stringent criteria for authenticity. His final collection contains around 7,275 hadiths (approximately 2,600 without repetition), organised into 97 books covering every domain of Islamic life — prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, marriage, warfare, commercial dealings, and the conduct of the Prophet.

His criteria for accepting a hadith included an unbroken chain of transmission (isnad) through narrators of proven integrity, and the requirement that each narrator in the chain had direct personal contact with the one before them. These standards were considered more rigorous than those of any of his contemporaries.

Part III — Source
Sahih Muslim

Sahih Muslim is the second most authoritative hadith collection in Sunni Islam, compiled by Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj al-Qushayri (815–875 CE), a scholar from Nishapur in present-day Iran. Together with Sahih al-Bukhari, it forms the Sahihayn — "the two Sahihs" — considered the gold standard of hadith literature across the Sunni world.

Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj studied under al-Bukhari himself, among many other scholars. He reportedly examined approximately 300,000 hadith traditions, retaining around 7,500 in his final collection. Unlike al-Bukhari's work, Sahih Muslim avoids repeating the same hadith under multiple chapter headings, and its organisation is considered more systematic — grouping traditions thematically rather than by legal topic alone.

Muslim scholars debate whether Sahih Muslim's isnad criteria were marginally stricter or looser than al-Bukhari's; the majority view ranks Bukhari first for authenticity, Muslim second. Both collections are accepted as binding in Sunni jurisprudence and cited routinely in Islamic legal rulings, sermons, and scholarship to this day.

Part I · The Quran · Cosmology xxx
"We sent down iron" — apologetic claim of meteoric origin
Cosmology Strange / Obscure Basic Q 57:25
"We sent down iron, wherein is great military might and benefits for the people."

What the verse says

The Quran uses the verb anzalna ("we sent down") for iron. Modern Islamic apologetics claims this anticipates the astrophysical discovery that heavy elements including iron are forged in supernovae and distributed to planetary systems — that iron was literally "sent down" from the stars.

Why this is a problem

The word anzalna is used throughout the Quran for scripture, rain, cattle, garments, and divine mercy — none of which originate in supernovae. It is the standard Quranic verb for divine provision or bestowal, used generically. The iron-from-supernova retrofit requires this common word to carry a meaning it carries nowhere else in the Quran. All heavy elements — carbon, oxygen, gold, uranium — also originate in stellar processes. The Quran does not say "we sent down carbon" or "we sent down oxygen." Singling out iron for the cosmic-origin reading is apologetic cherry-picking.

The Muslim response

The use of anzalna for iron is distinctive because the Quran uses it for objects with specific divine purposes rather than randomly. Iron's importance for human civilization (tools, weapons, agriculture) makes it a fitting candidate for a statement of divine provision that carries cosmological weight.

Why it fails

Anzalna is used for rain, cattle, and garments — items of obvious human utility — without those uses being interpreted as cosmological claims about stellar nucleosynthesis. The word means "we bestowed" or "we provided" across all its Quranic uses. Applying a unique astrophysical meaning only to iron, because modern science happens to support such a reading for iron specifically, is pattern-matching after the fact rather than linguistic analysis.

Strike the necks — "when you meet those who disbelieve, strike necks"
Warfare & Jihad Strong Q 47:4
"So when you meet those who disbelieve, strike necks until, when you have inflicted slaughter upon them, then secure their bonds."

What the verse says

Q 47:4 instructs believers to strike the necks of disbelievers in battle, continue until sufficient slaughter has been inflicted, then take prisoners. After the war lays down its burdens, prisoners may be released freely or ransomed.

Why this is a problem

The verse contains no defensive qualifier. It does not require that believers be attacked first, that a threat exist, or that any condition be met before striking. Classical commentators understood it as a general rule for battlefield conduct against unbelievers. The instruction to "inflict slaughter" before taking prisoners creates a hierarchy in which killing takes precedence over capture.

The Muslim response

Muslims argue that the verse describes battlefield conduct once combat has begun — it is not a licence to initiate violence, and must be read alongside verses that permit peace when the enemy inclines to it (Q 8:61) and that prohibit transgression (Q 2:190).

Why it fails

The verse does not say "if they attack you, strike." It says "when you meet those who disbelieve, strike." The meeting itself is the trigger. The phrase "strike necks" became the most literal possible instruction in classical jihad law precisely because the text provides no limiting principle that peaceable verses can override.

Banu Qurayza — the massacre the Quran does not condemn
Warfare & Jihad Moderate Q 33:26–27
"And He brought down those who supported them among the People of the Scripture from their fortresses and cast terror into their hearts; a party you killed, and you took captive a party."

What the verse says

Q 33:26–27 describes Allah bringing down the Banu Qurayza from their fortresses, casting terror into their hearts, after which some were killed and others taken captive. Between 600 and 900 men were executed; the women and children were enslaved.

Why this is a problem

The Quran does not merely record these events — it frames them as divine action ("He brought down," "He cast terror") and divine approval. Allah is the grammatical subject of both verbs. The massacre is not an event the Quran tolerates or regrets; it is an event the Quran attributes to Allah and presents as a gift to the believers.
