Part I — Source
The Quran

The Quran is the central scripture of Islam, believed by Muslims to be the literal, verbatim word of Allah as revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibreel (Gabriel) over a period of approximately twenty-three years, beginning around 610 CE and concluding at Muhammad's death in 632 CE.

Muhammad himself could not write. The revelations were memorised by companions and written on whatever materials were available — bones, leather, palm leaves. Following Muhammad's death, the first caliph Abu Bakr ordered a written collection to preserve the text. A standardised version was later produced under the third caliph Uthman ibn Affan around 650 CE. Uthman ordered all variant manuscripts burned, establishing a single authoritative text — an act that remains theologically significant and historically contested.

The Quran is divided into 114 chapters (surahs), each composed of individual verses (ayat). The surahs are arranged roughly from longest to shortest, not chronologically. Islamic scholarship divides them into Meccan revelations (earlier, more theological) and Medinan revelations (later, more legislative and often harsher in tone). Where the two conflict, the doctrine of abrogation (naskh) holds that the later verse supersedes the earlier.

All verses in this volume are quoted from the Saheeh International English translation — the Saudi-sanctioned mainstream Sunni edition used across English-speaking mosques and Islamic universities worldwide.

Source Introduction — Sahih al-Bukhari
Part II — Source
Sahih al-Bukhari

Sahih al-Bukhari is the most authoritative hadith collection in Sunni Islam, compiled by Muhammad ibn Ismail al-Bukhari (810–870 CE), a Persian scholar born in Bukhara in present-day Uzbekistan. Sunni Muslims regard it as the most authentic book after the Quran itself — a status it has held for over a thousand years.

Al-Bukhari reportedly examined approximately 600,000 hadith traditions over sixteen years of travel across the Islamic world, retaining only those he judged to meet his stringent criteria for authenticity. His final collection contains around 7,275 hadiths (approximately 2,600 without repetition), organised into 97 books covering every domain of Islamic life — prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, marriage, warfare, commercial dealings, and the conduct of the Prophet.

His criteria for accepting a hadith included an unbroken chain of transmission (isnad) through narrators of proven integrity, and the requirement that each narrator in the chain had direct personal contact with the one before them. These standards were considered more rigorous than those of any of his contemporaries.

Part III — Source
Sahih Muslim

Sahih Muslim is the second most authoritative hadith collection in Sunni Islam, compiled by Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj al-Qushayri (815–875 CE), a scholar from Nishapur in present-day Iran. Together with Sahih al-Bukhari, it forms the Sahihayn — "the two Sahihs" — considered the gold standard of hadith literature across the Sunni world.

Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj studied under al-Bukhari himself, among many other scholars. He reportedly examined approximately 300,000 hadith traditions, retaining around 7,500 in his final collection. Unlike al-Bukhari's work, Sahih Muslim avoids repeating the same hadith under multiple chapter headings, and its organisation is considered more systematic — grouping traditions thematically rather than by legal topic alone.

Muslim scholars debate whether Sahih Muslim's isnad criteria were marginally stricter or looser than al-Bukhari's; the majority view ranks Bukhari first for authenticity, Muslim second. Both collections are accepted as binding in Sunni jurisprudence and cited routinely in Islamic legal rulings, sermons, and scholarship to this day.
