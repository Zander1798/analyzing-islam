Argument 13 of 20 · Sunan an-Nasā'ī

Donkey/Devil Cosmology (Nasa'i Wording)

Nasa'i 1623 (parallel Bukhari 3303)
The text
Nasa'i 1623 — Records the same teaching as Bukhari 3303 (entry b16): cocks crow at angels, donkeys bray at devils. The Nasa'i version provides cross-collection attestation for the canonical animal-spirit cosmology.
Context

Nasa'i 1623 reinforces the cock-and-donkey hadith treated under Bukhari 3303 (entry b16). The substantive issues are addressed there. The Nasa'i contribution: cross-collection attestation confirms the teaching is canonical Sunni doctrine, preserved across multiple major collections. The same teaching also appears in Muslim 2729, giving the hadith a three-collection footprint spanning the two most authoritative Sunni compilations (Bukhari 3303; Muslim 2729) and Nasa'i 1623.

Jonathan A.C. Brown, The Canonization of al-Bukhārī and Muslim (Brill, 2007), traces how reports reaching both al-Bukhari and Muslim were regarded by the Sunni scholarly tradition as carrying the strongest possible evidential weight; the further corroboration from Nasa'i only deepens that standing. Brown's later work, Misquoting Muhammad (Oneworld, 2014), notes that canonically-graded hadiths — including those in the top-tier collections — function as binding doctrinal authority for Sunni Muslims, not merely as historical curiosities. The cock-and-donkey tradition therefore cannot be dismissed as a marginal or isolated report.

Classical commentators took the teaching literally. Ibn Hajar al-Asqalanī, in his magnum opus Fath al-Bari (his commentary on Sahih al-Bukhari, completed 15th century CE), explains that roosters possess a faculty of sight that perceives angels, so the believer is instructed to request God's favour at the moment of crowing; donkeys conversely perceive rebellious jinn (shaytan), so the believer is to seek refuge from the devil at the moment of braying. Ibn Hajar treats the animal perception as literal fact, not metaphor — a position transmitted through the mainstream classical commentary tradition.

Silas, writing for answering-islam.org ("Muhammad and Satan"), observes that zoology and ethology provide no support for the claim that rooster crowing or donkey braying tracks the presence of supernatural entities; the behaviour of both animals is fully explained by circadian biology and social signalling respectively. The hadith's causal claim is therefore empirically falsifiable and, on current evidence, false.

Notably, the Egyptian modernist critic Mahmoud Abu Rayyah — whose principal work Adwa' `ala al-Sunna al-Muhammadiyya (1958) argued that any hadith contradicting reason or natural science must be judged weak — represents an internal Islamic tradition of dissent that is significant precisely because it does not originate from outside the faith. His rationalist methodology, which drew on the reformist heritage of Muhammad Abduh, is directly applicable to the cock-and-donkey teaching as a canonical report that makes an empirically falsifiable causal claim.

The analysis from entry b16 applies fully here.

Premises
  1. P1. Nasa'i 1623 preserves the cock-and-donkey hadith with content paralleling Bukhari 3303.
  2. P2. The hadith is in multiple canonical collections — Bukhari 3303, Muslim 2729, and Nasa'i 1623 — giving it the highest degree of cross-collection consistency in Sunni hadith science (Jonathan A.C. Brown, The Canonization of al-Bukhārī and Muslim, Brill, 2007).
  3. P3. The teaching attributes animal vocalisations to perception of supernatural beings — a pre-scientific cosmology taken literally by classical commentators including Ibn Hajar al-Asqalanī in Fath al-Bari.
  4. P4. The framework reflects 7th-century Arabian folk-cosmology, not divine teaching about biology; answering-islam.org (Silas, "Muhammad and Satan") notes that neither ethology nor zoology supports the causal claim.
  5. P5. The teaching generates ritual responses (asking blessings on cock-crow, seeking refuge on donkey-bray) still practised by Muslims, as the formula is preserved in classical Adhkar literature.
  6. P6. The framework is part of a broader pattern of canonical hadith treating natural phenomena as supernatural agents.
  7. P7. An omniscient God would not teach a final prophet that animal vocalisations are responses to invisible supernatural entities. (See entry b16.)
Conclusion

Nasa'i 1623 reinforces the cock-and-donkey hadith with cross-collection attestation. (See entry b16 for substantive analysis.)

Common Muslim responses · with counter-responses
Common Muslim response · 1

The hadith reflects Muhammad's spiritual insight into creation.

Counter-response

The empirical claim is unsupported by ethology. Silas, writing for answering-islam.org ("Muhammad and Satan"), catalogues the absence of any biological basis for the causal account. A claim of spiritual insight cannot be immunised from scrutiny by labelling the empirical claim metaphorical — classical commentators, including Ibn Hajar in Fath al-Bari, took it literally. (See also entry b16.)

Common Muslim response · 2

The hadith is merely a mindfulness teaching device, not a literal scientific claim.

Counter-response

Pedagogical intent and propositional falsity are not mutually exclusive. Ibn Hajar al-Asqalanī's Fath al-Bari treats the animal-perception claim as a literal fact and grounds the ritual du'a in it. If the factual premise is false, the ritual instruction is also misfounded. Jonathan A.C. Brown, Misquoting Muhammad (Oneworld, 2014), documents how classical scholars consistently applied such hadiths as binding doctrine, not loose metaphor. (See b16.)

Common Muslim response · 3

Science cannot rule out angels and devils.

Counter-response

The issue is not whether angels and devils exist but whether rooster crowing reliably tracks their presence. That causal claim is empirically testable: roosters crow on circadian rhythms regardless of supernatural context. The claim is therefore not unfalsifiable metaphysics but a falsifiable statement about animal behaviour — and it is false. (See b16.)

Common Muslim response · 4

Ancient wisdom we don't fully understand.

Counter-response

Unfalsifiable framing: any falsified prediction can be insulated by claiming hidden wisdom. This move is unavailable to a tradition that grounds authority on hadith precision and chain-criticism. Mahmoud Abu Rayyah, the Egyptian modernist whose Adwa' `ala al-Sunna (1958) applied rationalist and scientific criteria to hadith evaluation, represents an internal Islamic tradition that treats empirically falsifiable claims in hadith as grounds for rejection — evidence that the "hidden wisdom" defence is not universally accepted even within the faith. (See b16.)

Common Muslim response · 5

Minor matters not affecting core doctrine.

Counter-response

The teaching is canonical (Bukhari 3303, Muslim 2729, and Nasa'i 1623 all attest it), is explained literally by the foremost classical hadith commentator Ibn Hajar, and generates still-practised ritual obligations. Brown, The Canonization of al-Bukhārī and Muslim (Brill, 2007), shows why reports at this level of attestation cannot be reclassified as minor. The issue affects core epistemological methodology — if a multi-attested, classically-affirmed teaching is scientifically false, that is a methodological problem for the whole tradition, not a marginal one. (See b16.)
