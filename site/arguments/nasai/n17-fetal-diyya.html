Argument 17 of 20 · Sunan an-Nasā'ī

Diyya for Fetus (Unequal Valuation)

Nasa'i 4099
The text
Nasai 4099 — Various Nasa'i hadith on diyya for fetal injury or death (diyat al-janīn). The classical framework: a slave (ghurra — typically a young slave girl or boy worth a specified amount) is owed for the loss of a fetus due to assault on the mother. The amount varies by gestational age, viability, and gender of the fetus. The Quranic anchor (Q 4:92 — diyya generally) does not specify fetal diyya; the hadith and classical fiqh elaborate.
Context

Nasa'i 4099 records the canonical framework for fetal blood-money. The framework treats a fetus as having legal-financial value but at a reduced level compared to a born person. Joseph Schacht, An Introduction to Islamic Law (Clarendon Press, 1964), the standard Western reference for classical fiqh, notes that the law of compensation (diyya) is among the most elaborately developed areas of Islamic criminal doctrine, with graduated rules covering not only adults but the unborn. The compensation varies by:

1. Gestational age. Earlier-stage miscarriages receive less compensation than later-stage. 2. Gender. Some classical schools distinguish male and female fetuses, with female fetuses valued less. 3. Viability. After ensoulment (typically at 120 days per the angel-writes-four-things hadith), the fetus is treated as a person and may receive full diyya.

The theological problems:

1. Tiered fetal personhood. The framework establishes that fetuses have graded legal-financial value depending on developmental stage. This is itself an ethical claim about when human life begins to matter morally — and the answer is 'gradually,' not 'at conception.'

2. The 120-day ensoulment. The standard Sunni position is that ensoulment occurs at 120 days (per the angel-writes-four-things hadith — Bukhari 3208; parallel: Muslim 2643). The hadith records that after three forty-day stages (nutfa, 'alaqa, mudgha), the angel is sent to breathe the soul into the embryo and write four things: provision, deeds, lifespan, and fate. Ibn Hajar al-'Asqalani, in his commentary Fath al-Bari (the canonical Arabic commentary on Sahih al-Bukhari), draws on this hadith to establish that voluntary movement and perception — the markers of full moral status — are acquired only after ensoulment. Before ensoulment, the fetus has reduced moral status. This affects abortion ethics in Islamic legal tradition: pre-120-day abortions are often permitted under specific circumstances, post-120-day abortions are generally forbidden.

3. Gender differential. Some classical schools (especially Maliki) value male fetuses higher than female fetuses for diyya purposes. This extends the gender halving (entry d05) to the fetal stage, creating the most extreme intersectional reduction: female fetuses at the lowest legal-financial value. Rudolph Peters, Crime and Punishment in Islamic Law: Theory and Practice from the Sixteenth to the Twenty-First Century (Cambridge University Press, 2005), the leading modern survey of Islamic criminal law in practice, documents how diyya rules — including differentiated compensation rates — remained operative in Ottoman courts and modern Muslim-majority states well into the contemporary period.

4. The ghurra payment. The classical compensation is a 'ghurra' — typically a slave girl or boy of specified value. Malik ibn Anas preserves the earliest systematic ruling in the Muwatta, Book 43, Hadith 6 (Blood-Money), where the Prophet's judgment in a case involving a woman of the Hudhayl tribe is recorded: a stone thrown at a pregnant woman caused a miscarriage, and the ruling was that the diyya for the fetus is a ghurra — a slave or slave-girl of fair complexion and excellence. This ruling is preserved through Abu Hurayra via Ibn Shihab. This is itself problematic: the compensation for human life lost is paid in the form of another human (slave). The framework treats some humans (slaves) as currency for compensating for the loss of other humans (fetuses).

5. Modern application. Modern Saudi Arabia, Iran, and other Muslim-majority states have applied versions of fetal-diyya rules. Peters (2005) documents that the frameworks have been adapted — typically converting ghurra into monetary equivalents — but generally preserve the principle of tiered fetal personhood.

6. Comparison with modern medical ethics. Modern bioethics generally treats personhood as developing through gestation (with various positions on when full moral status applies). The Islamic framework's specifics — 120-day ensoulment, gender differential, ghurra-payment — are pre-modern and reflect 7th-century legal-medical understanding.

7. The internal Muslim divergence. Sunni and Shia jurisprudence vary on fetal diyya specifics. The fragmentation reflects the framework's contested nature.

Premises
  1. P1. Nasa'i 4099 records the canonical framework for fetal diyya.
  2. P2. The framework treats fetuses as having graded legal-financial value depending on developmental stage.
  3. P3. The 120-day ensoulment standard establishes a gradient of moral status during pregnancy.
  4. P4. Classical schools vary on whether male and female fetuses receive different compensation, with some applying gender halving.
  5. P5. The classical compensation form (ghurra — a slave) treats slave humans as currency for compensating fetal loss.
  6. P6. Modern Muslim-majority states apply versions of the framework with various adaptations.
  7. P7. A morally serious framework does not establish tiered moral status for fetuses by developmental stage and gender.
Conclusion

Nasa'i 4099 anchors the canonical Islamic fetal-diyya framework. The system's tiered fetal personhood, gender differential (in some schools), and ghurra-payment structure reflect 7th-century legal-medical understanding rather than divine teaching about human moral status. Modern Muslim apologetic responses face the difficulty that the textual basis is detailed and the consequences (graded fetal valuation) are operative. The text is what we would expect of pre-modern legal-economic compensation calibrated to its cultural moment, and exactly what we would not expect of a divine teaching about the value of nascent human life.

Common Muslim responses · with counter-responses
Common Muslim response · 1

The fetal-diyya framework recognises that fetuses develop morally — this is a sophisticated graduated approach to fetal personhood.

Counter-response

The graduated approach reflects 7th-century medical-legal understanding. Modern bioethics has its own debates on fetal personhood, but the specific Islamic framework (120-day ensoulment, ghurra payment) is calibrated to its time. Joseph Schacht, An Introduction to Islamic Law (1964), observed that Islamic compensation law developed as a legal-economic system of inter-tribal liability, not as a theological meditation on personhood. The 'sophisticated graduated approach' framing minimises the framework's specifically pre-modern character.

Common Muslim response · 2

The 120-day ensoulment is consistent with various medical observations of fetal development — Islam was scientifically advanced.

Counter-response

The 120-day specification matches no specific empirical milestone. Modern fetal development knowledge does not endorse 120 days as a uniquely significant transition. The specification is religious-traditional, not empirically grounded.

Common Muslim response · 3

The ghurra payment was a culturally appropriate compensation in a slave society — modern application uses monetary equivalents.

Counter-response

Concedes the slave-as-currency framing was culturally appropriate, which is itself the problem. The framework treated humans as compensable units, including in the case of fetal loss. Modern monetary equivalents do not undo the original conceptual structure.

Common Muslim response · 4

Modern Muslim states are reforming fetal-diyya rules.

Counter-response

Reform is uneven. Rudolph Peters, Crime and Punishment in Islamic Law (Cambridge University Press, 2005), documents that while modern Muslim-majority states convert the ghurra into monetary equivalents, the graduated fetal valuation principle — and the gender differential in some jurisdictions — persists in statutory codes and court decisions across the Gulf and Iran.

Common Muslim response · 5

Modern abortion ethics also has graduated fetal status — Islamic position is not unique.

Counter-response

Modern bioethics does have graduated approaches, but the Islamic specific framework includes gender differential, ghurra payment, and 120-day ensoulment that are not paralleled in modern bioethics. The cross-tradition observation does not redeem the specific Islamic framework.
