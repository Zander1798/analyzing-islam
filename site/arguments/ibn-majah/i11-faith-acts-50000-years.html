Argument 11 of 20 · Sunan Ibn Mājah

Faith and Acts Written 50,000 Years Before Creation

Ibn Majah 79 (parallels t05, m09)
The text
Ibn Majah 79 — Records the canonical predestination teaching: Allah wrote all decrees fifty thousand years before creating the heavens and the earth. The hadith parallels Tirmidhi 2516 (entry t05) and Muslim 2664.
Context

Ibn Majah 79 reinforces the foundational predestination hadith treated under entries t05 and m09. The Ibn Majah contribution: cross-collection attestation in the credal-doctrinal section of Ibn Majah's collection, confirming the teaching as foundational creed. The parallel text in Muslim 2664 reads: "Allah wrote down the decrees of creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth, while His throne was upon water." Its placement across three canonical collections — Tirmidhi, Muslim, and Ibn Majah — means the teaching carries the highest level of cross-attestation in Sunni hadith science.

The substantive issues are addressed in entries t05, m09, t01, t19. The Ibn Majah specific addition: the Sunan placement in the iman (faith) chapter establishes the predestination framework as part of Islamic creed, not just legal-procedural detail. W. Montgomery Watt, in Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam (London: Luzac, 1948), traces the tension between divine omnipotence and human responsibility back to the earliest post-Quranic theological controversies, noting that the fatalistic implications of the qadar hadiths were recognised by the Qadariyya opponents of strict predestination as early as the late first century AH — well before systematic kalam developed a vocabulary to manage them.

The Mutazilite school, which championed human free will on grounds of divine justice, rejected the deterministic reading of such hadiths. When the Asharite school consolidated Sunni orthodoxy in the tenth century, it produced the doctrine of kasb (acquisition) to square the circle: Allah creates the act; the human merely "acquires" it. Watt, in The Formative Period of Islamic Thought (Edinburgh University Press, 1973), documents how the Mutazilite–Asharite controversy was never fully resolved at the philosophical level, only institutionally settled in favour of the Asharite position. Eric L. Ormsby, in Theodicy in Islamic Thought: The Dispute over al-Ghazali's 'Best of All Possible Worlds' (Princeton University Press, 1984), examines al-Ghazali's theodicy — the most sophisticated internal attempt to reconcile divine omnipotence with the existence of evil — and shows that al-Ghazali's system commits him to the view that every occurrence, good and evil alike, is foreordained by a prior divine volition and could not have been otherwise; the vocabulary of human acquisition sits within a framework of exhaustive divine determination.

The analysis from those entries applies fully here.

Premises
  1. P1. Ibn Majah 79 records the 50,000-year predestination teaching with creed-genre placement.
  2. P2. The hadith is preserved across multiple canonical collections.
  3. P3. The framework establishes maximally strict predestination as part of Islamic creed.
  4. P4. Combined with the angel-writes-four-things hadith, the system fixes outcomes at multiple temporal layers.
  5. P5. The Asharite kasb doctrine has produced verbal compromises but not metaphysical resolutions. Watt, Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam (1948), argues the controversy was institutionally settled rather than philosophically resolved. Ormsby, Theodicy in Islamic Thought (Princeton University Press, 1984), shows that al-Ghazali's theodicy commits him to exhaustive divine foreordination of every occurrence, embedding the determinism problem within the very system designed to transcend it.
  6. P6. The Mu'tazilite-Asharite controversy in classical Islam reflects the tension within the tradition. Watt, The Formative Period of Islamic Thought (Edinburgh University Press, 1973), documents the Qadariyya as the earliest explicit critics of the fatalist implications of the qadar hadiths.
  7. P7. A coherent moral theology cannot affirm exhaustive divine pre-determination at multiple levels and meaningful human responsibility. (See entries t05, m09, t01, t19.)
Conclusion

Ibn Majah 79 places the 50,000-year predestination teaching in the creed section of the canonical Sunan. The framework is foundational Islamic creed. W. Montgomery Watt, Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam (London: Luzac, 1948), established that the tension between this tradition and any robust doctrine of human moral responsibility was not an oversight of naive transmitters but a recognised theological problem from the earliest period of Islamic kalam — one that the Asharite synthesis domesticated institutionally without dissolving philosophically. Eric L. Ormsby, Theodicy in Islamic Thought (Princeton University Press, 1984), demonstrates that even al-Ghazali's theodicy — the most intellectually ambitious resolution attempted within classical Sunni thought — requires exhaustive divine foreordination of every occurrence, leaving no genuine alternative course available to human agents. The creedal canonisation of this hadith therefore perpetuates an unresolved problem at the heart of Islamic moral theology. (See entries t05, m09, t01, t19 for substantive analysis.)

Common Muslim responses · with counter-responses
Common Muslim response · 1

Foreknowledge expressed temporally.

Counter-response

The text does not say Allah knew decrees fifty thousand years in advance; it says Allah wrote (kataba) them — the verb of inscription used throughout the Quran for binding divine legislation (Q 2:183; Q 5:32). W. Montgomery Watt, Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam (London: Luzac, 1948), notes that the early Qadariyya critics of strict predestination directed their objections precisely at the writing imagery — not because it was metaphorical, but because the tradition deployed it as determinative legislation, not merely foreknowledge. (See also t05.)

Common Muslim response · 2

Asharite kasb resolves contradiction.

Counter-response

Eric L. Ormsby, Theodicy in Islamic Thought (Princeton University Press, 1984), examines al-Ghazali's theodicy — the most sophisticated Asharite framework — and demonstrates that al-Ghazali's system requires every event, including moral choices, to be divinely foreordained and incapable of being otherwise. Within that framework, the kasb formula says a human "acquires" what God creates; but if the acquisition itself is also divinely determined, the agency attributed to the human is nominal. Ormsby's analysis of al-Ghazali's voluntarism shows that the systematic coherence of al-Ghazali's position depends on a divine will that leaves no alternative course open. This is not a resolution of the determinism problem; it is an elaborated version of it. (See t05.)

Common Muslim response · 5

Other traditions also wrestle with similar issues.

Counter-response

The Islamic predestination framework is unusually specific in its temporal and material claims: not a philosophical assertion about divine omniscience, but a dated, inscribed act fifty thousand years before creation involving a physical Pen and Preserved Tablet (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz). Watt, The Formative Period of Islamic Thought (Edinburgh University Press, 1973), notes that the hadith tradition provides a level of specificity — temporal, instrumental, and creedal — that distinguishes Islamic qadar from Calvinist predestination or Stoic fate, making it harder to allegorise. The teaching appears not as peripheral speculation but as a pillar of iman in the Hadith of Gabriel (Muslim 8). (See t05.)
