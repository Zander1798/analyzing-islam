Argument 10 of 20 · The Qur'ān

The Sun Setting in a Muddy Spring

Q 18:86
The text
Q 18:86 — "Until, when he reached the setting of the sun, he found it [as if] setting in a spring of dark mud, and he found near it a people. We said, 'O Dhu al-Qarnayn, either you punish [them] or else adopt among them [a way of] goodness.'"
Context

Surah 18 (al-Kahf) recounts the legend of Dhul-Qarnayn ('the two-horned one'), a righteous king who travels to the ends of the earth. The narrative parallels (and almost certainly adapts) the Syriac Christian Alexander Romance (the Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs), a 6th-century legendary biography of Alexander the Great, which was widely circulated in the Near East before Muhammad's time. Kevin van Bladel, in 'The Alexander Legend in the Qur'an 18:83–102' (in Gabriel Said Reynolds, ed., The Qur'an in Its Historical Context, Routledge, 2008, pp. 175–202), demonstrates that every stage of the Quranic passage has a close parallel in the Syriac legend and often in the same order: the righteous-king traveler, the journey to the sun's setting, the journey to the sun's rising, the encounter with a barbarous people, and the construction of a barrier against Gog and Magog. Most decisively, the water at the place where the sun sets is described as 'fetid/black' in both texts — the Syriac saryā and the Arabic ḥamiʾa are cognate synonyms, a near-impossible coincidence if the Quran were independently composed. The Syriac legend describes Alexander sailing until he reaches Oceanus, the fetid sea which surrounds the earth, and seeing the sun sink 'in a pool of mud and fetid slime.' Tommaso Tesei, in The Syriac Legend of Alexander's Gate: Apocalypticism at the Crossroads of Byzantium and Iran (Oxford University Press, 2023), independently corroborates van Bladel's thesis, arguing that an earlier stratum of the Syriac text was composed under Justinian I (r. 527–565) and that the Quranic account shares multiple streams of narrative elements from this tradition in the same sequence — further tightening the case for direct literary dependency.

Q 18:86 says Dhul-Qarnayn 'reached the setting of the sun' (maghriba l-shams) and 'found it setting in a spring of dark/muddy water' (taghrubu fī ʿaynin ḥamiʾah). The Arabic ʿayn is unambiguously a water-spring; ḥamiʾah means 'muddy' or 'black' (a textual variant reading is ḥāmiya — 'hot'). Both readings describe a physical location on earth where the sun physically sets into a body of water.

Classical tafsir treated the description as substantially geographical. Al-Tabari, in his Jāmiʿ al-Bayān ʿan Taʾwīl Āy al-Qurʾān, preserves multiple transmitted reports identifying the location as a real destination on the western edge of the inhabited world; while he allows that the scene reflects what Dhul-Qarnayn observed, he still transmits these geographical reports without dismissing the physical imagery. Ibn Kathir, in his Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm, similarly transmits these geographical reports — noting the spring was 'black, muddy and stinking' — and connects the verse directly to the hadith material about the sun prostrating beneath Allah's throne (Bukhari 3199), treating the setting-in-the-spring and the prostration as two aspects of the same cosmological framework. Al-Qurtubi, in his al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān, is notable for being among the earliest classical commentators to introduce a phenomenological hedge — he proposes that the sun set 'as if' in a spring from Dhul-Qarnayn's visual perspective, analogising it to a sailor who sees the sun appear to set in the sea — but this is precisely the apologetic move the verse's grammar does not support (see Common Muslim Response 1 below), and it shows that even classical mufassirs felt the cosmological pressure of the literal reading. The verse itself uses the indicative 'found it setting,' not 'appeared to him.' The cosmological reading is further anchored by Abu Dawud 4002, in which Muhammad himself tells Abu Dharr that the sun 'sets in a spring of warm water' — the Prophet citing Q 18:86 as literal geography. Q 18:90, parallel to 18:86, says Dhul-Qarnayn 'reached the rising of the sun' and 'found it rising on a people' — the same physicalist framework.

The astronomical fact: the sun does not set into anything. It is a star approximately 150 million kilometres from Earth, around which Earth orbits. Its 'setting' is a perspectival illusion produced by Earth's rotation. There is no spring, lake, or sea on Earth into which the sun descends. The verse describes a flat-earth cosmology in which the sun has a fixed daily destination on the planetary surface — a worldview common in pre-modern Near Eastern myth (Egyptian Ra in his solar barque, Greek Helios in his chariot) and incompatible with basic astronomy.

This verse cannot be defended as poetic without conceding that other verses (e.g. Q 36:38, 'the sun runs to a resting place for it') and the parallel Hadith literature (Bukhari 3199, where the sun prostrates beneath Allah's throne) construct a coherent flat-earth solar cosmology. The 'poetic' defence requires that we know in advance which verses are literal and which are figurative — a knowledge available only after modern astronomy.

Premises
  1. P1. Q 18:86 states that Dhul-Qarnayn found the sun setting in a muddy/black water-spring — describing a physical location reached by travel.
  2. P2. The verse uses indicative-mood verbs ('he found it setting,' wajadahā taghrubu) without subjunctive or hedging language.
  3. P3. Astronomically, the sun does not set into any earthly body of water; it is a star ~150 million km from Earth, and its apparent setting is caused by Earth's rotation.
  4. P4. The Quranic narrative borrows directly from the 6th-century Syriac Alexander Legend (Neṣḥānā d-Aleksandrōs), which contains the same flat-earth imagery (sun setting in a fetid sea, rising over a shelterless people); the textual dependence is established by Kevin van Bladel, 'The Alexander Legend in the Qur'an 18:83–102,' in Reynolds, ed., The Qur'an in Its Historical Context (Routledge, 2008), pp. 175–202, and corroborated by Tommaso Tesei, The Syriac Legend of Alexander's Gate (Oxford University Press, 2023).
  5. P4a. Abu Dawud 4002 records Muhammad citing Q 18:86 as literal geography, telling Abu Dharr at sunset that the sun 'sets in a spring of warm water' — evidence that the Prophet's own exegesis was physicalist, not phenomenological.
  6. P5. Classical tafsir treated the verse as describing a real geographical location, not a poetic vision.
  7. P6. Q 36:38 ('the sun runs to a resting place for it') and parallel hadiths (Bukhari 3199) reinforce a flat-earth solar cosmology, indicating the 'spring' verse is not an isolated metaphor.
  8. P7. An omniscient God would not narrate a cosmology that contradicts basic physical reality, especially while claiming the book as universally true (Q 41:42).
Conclusion

Q 18:86 describes the sun physically setting in a muddy spring, which is impossible. The verse is best explained as Muhammad's adaptation of a pre-Islamic Christian legend that was circulating in his region, complete with that legend's pre-modern cosmology. The 'poetic' or 'visual appearance' defence is post-hoc and inconsistent with the verse's grammar, with classical exegesis, and with parallel Quranic and hadith material. The verse is exactly what we would expect of a seventh-century Arabian text borrowing from Syriac apocrypha, and not what we would expect of a divine revelation from the maker of the cosmos.

Common Muslim responses · with counter-responses
Common Muslim response · 1

The verse describes Dhul-Qarnayn's perception, not the actual physical setting of the sun — it 'appeared to him' that way.

Counter-response

The verse uses wajadahā taghrubu ('he found it setting'), not ka-annahā taghrubu ('as if it were setting'). Classical Arabic distinguishes the two clearly. Tabari and Ibn Kathir do not read this as merely apparent. And the parallel verse Q 18:90, describing the rising, uses the same construction — meaning we'd have to apply the 'apparent' reading universally and conclude that Dhul-Qarnayn's whole journey to 'the rising' and 'the setting' was hallucinated. If so, the verse is uninformative. More decisively, Abu Dawud 4002 records Muhammad himself telling Abu Dharr that the sun 'sets in a spring of warm water' — the Prophet's own first-person gloss on this passage was not phenomenological but literal, foreclosing the 'mere perception' reading as a revisionist apology.

Common Muslim response · 2

The story is symbolic — Dhul-Qarnayn represents the ideal righteous king, and the imagery is poetic.

Counter-response

Symbolic readings require some textual marker — a parable formula ('like one who...'), a prefatory frame, a context signalling allegory. Q 18:83–98 is presented as a historical narrative ('they ask you about Dhul-Qarnayn... say: I will recount to you about him'). The verse names a real person, places him on a journey, and describes his actions in factual prose. There is no symbolic frame. Furthermore, classical tafsir spent enormous effort identifying who Dhul-Qarnayn 'really was' (Alexander, a Persian king, an Arab king) — incoherent if the figure were merely symbolic.

Common Muslim response · 3

ʿAyn here means 'horizon' or 'sight,' not 'spring' — the sun set 'in his sight.'

Counter-response

ʿAyn does have the secondary meaning 'eye/sight,' but in this verse it is qualified as ḥamiʾah (muddy/black) — an adjective for water, not for sight. 'A muddy horizon' is not a meaningful phrase. Classical Arabic lexicons (Lisān al-ʿArab) and every classical mufassir read ʿayn here as a spring of water. The 'horizon' reading is a 20th-century apologetic invention.

Common Muslim response · 4

The Alexander Romance parallel is overstated — there are differences between the Syriac legend and Q 18.

Counter-response

There are minor narrative differences but the structural parallels are extensive: the righteous-king-traveler, the journey to the sun's setting, the journey to the sun's rising, the encounter with a barbarous people, the construction of a barrier against Gog and Magog, the presence of a body of water at the sun's setting. The Syriac legend predates Q 18 by approximately a century and was demonstrably circulating in the Near East. Even Muslim scholars (e.g. Wahb ibn Munabbih in the early Islamic period) acknowledged the Alexander connection. The textual borrowing is established by Kevin van Bladel, 'The Alexander Legend in the Qur'an 18:83–102,' in Gabriel Said Reynolds, ed., The Qur'an in Its Historical Context (Routledge, 2008), pp. 175–202, whose key philological argument is that the water at the sun's setting is described as 'fetid/black' in both texts — the Syriac saryā and Arabic ḥamiʾa being cognate synonyms, an extremely unlikely coincidence if the texts were independent. Tommaso Tesei's The Syriac Legend of Alexander's Gate (Oxford University Press, 2023) independently corroborates the dependence, tracing the Neshana's composition to pre-Islamic Byzantium and documenting multiple narrative streams shared with Surah 18 in the same order.

Common Muslim response · 5

The verse is consistent with how the sun appears at the horizon over an ocean — anyone watching a sunset over water sees it 'setting in' the water.

Counter-response

This is the 'apparent' defence again, repackaged. The verse names a specific location (a muddy spring with people living near it) reached by travel, not the universal experience of watching a sunset. Dhul-Qarnayn 'reached' (balagha) the place where the sun sets — not just a coastline. The verse describes a destination, not a viewpoint. And no apologetic that defends the sunset-on-the-horizon experience explains the corresponding 'rising' verse, which describes the sun rising on a people who 'have no shelter' from it (Q 18:90) — coherent only on a flat-earth model.
